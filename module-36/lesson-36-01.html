🎵 PART 1 · WHAT IS A DAW?

A DAW (Digital Audio Workstation) is software that lets you record, arrange, and mix sound on a computer. Think of it as a recording studio that fits on a laptop. Common free DAWs include GarageBand (Mac), BandLab (browser/app), and Soundtrap (browser). A DAW has three main areas: the Track List, the Timeline, and the Transport.

  • The Track List runs down the left side. Each row is one track: one instrument, one vocal line, one drum part.
  • The Timeline (also called the Arrangement view) runs to the right. Blocks of sound sit on a grid. Moving them left or right changes when they play.
  • The Transport bar has the controls you already know from a music player: Play, Stop, Record, and the position marker that shows where you are in the song.

🎶 PART 2 · MIDI VS. AUDIO

Two very different kinds of data live in a DAW: MIDI and audio.

Audio is a recording of actual sound: a microphone captures air pressure and stores it as a waveform. When you record yourself singing or play a guitar into a microphone, you get audio. Audio sounds exactly like the original performance. But it is not easy to edit: to change one wrong note, you re-record.

MIDI stands for Musical Instrument Digital Interface. MIDI does not store sound. It stores instructions: which note, how long, how loud. When a MIDI file plays, the computer reads those instructions and uses a software instrument to make the sound. MIDI is very editable: wrong notes, tempo, instrument sound -- all changeable after the fact.

🎶 MIDI vs. AUDIO: Side by Side
MIDI
Piano Roll (note events)
✓ Stores instructions (note on/off, pitch, velocity)
✓ Easy to edit: change any note, tempo, or instrument
✓ No sound until it hits a software instrument
AUDIO
Audio Waveform
✓ Stores actual sound waves (air pressure data)
✓ Plays back exactly as recorded
✓ Harder to edit: one wrong note means re-recording

🎹 PART 3 · NOTATION SOFTWARE VS. DAWs

MuseScore (free, musescore.org) is notation software, not a DAW. It shows music the way a printed score looks: staves, noteheads, rests. Use MuseScore when you want to compose, print, or share written sheet music. Use a DAW when you want to produce, record, or mix sound for listening. Both tools are powerful. They are designed for different goals.

🎥 PART 4 · HOW TECHNOLOGY CHANGED MUSIC

Technology changed music in four connected ways.

Creation:
You no longer need a full band or a recording studio. One person with a laptop can produce a complete track.
Performance:
Many live shows use backing tracks, looped samples, and electronic instruments alongside acoustic ones.
Distribution:
In 1980 you had to buy a physical record; today a song can reach millions of listeners the day it is finished.
Experience:
Streaming means listeners have access to more music than any library ever held; but it also means artists earn far less per play than they did from album sales. Each change has benefits and trade-offs worth thinking about.
⚖ PART 5 · RESPONSIBLE USE AND COPYRIGHT

When you use technology to make music, you quickly run into questions about whose work belongs to whom.

Recorded music is protected by copyright. If you sample (take a clip from) someone else's recording, you need permission. Using it without permission -- even a short clip -- is copyright infringement.

Chord progressions are NOT protected by copyright. The I-V-vi-IV progression (used in thousands of songs) belongs to no one; you can use any chord progression freely.

Melodies and lyrics ARE protected. Even if you change the chords or the tempo, using someone else's melody or lyrics without a license is infringement.

Original compositions you create from scratch belong to you. If you use a sample from a royalty-free library (many are free or low-cost), check the license: some require credit, some allow commercial use, some do not.

AI tools can help generate musical ideas. The legal status of AI-generated music is still being worked out in courts and in Congress. For now: if an AI tool generated a specific melody for you based on another artist's style, use it carefully; it does not automatically mean the result is license-free. The honest approach is to use AI assistance as a starting point, then make the work your own.

AI AND MUSIC TOOLS
Some DAW apps and online tools include AI features that can generate drum patterns, suggest chord voicings, or complete a melody. These tools can be useful for exploring ideas. AI cannot hear music the way you do; it works by pattern-matching from a large database of existing music. For any AI-assisted work, the same copyright principles apply: the source material the AI was trained on may be protected even if the AI output sounds original. When you present work as your own, be accurate about what tools you used.

💪 THE ONE THING TO REMEMBER

A DAW is a studio on a laptop. MIDI stores instructions; audio stores sound. Chord progressions are free; melodies, lyrics, and recordings are protected. Know the difference, and you can create confidently and honestly.
