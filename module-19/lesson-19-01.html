Hearing the Music First: Solfege and Sight-Singing

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How can a musician hear music before singing a single note?
A musician who can sight-sing walks up to a piece of sheet music and sings it correctly the first time. No recording needed. No one to show them the melody. Just the page and the voice. The secret is a system of syllables that connects written notes to sounds you already know in your body. Today you learn that system.

🎵 PART 1 · WHAT IS SIGHT-SINGING?
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you start: how often have you tried to sing along with sheet music you had never heard before?

Sight-singing is the ability to perform a piece of music at first reading, without having heard it played first. It is one of the most valuable practical skills a musician can develop. A musician who can sight-sing learns new music independently, auditions confidently, and participates in ensembles with less preparation time.

The foundation of sight-singing is audiation: a term coined by music educator Edwin Gordon. Audiation means mentally hearing a pitch relationship before producing it with your voice. Think of it like reading text silently before speaking it aloud. The more you practice reading melodies and then singing them, the more automatic your internal pitch sense becomes.

💡 KEY TERMS
Sight-singing: Performing written music at first reading, without prior rehearsal.
Audiation: Hearing music internally before producing it with your voice (term from Edwin Gordon).
Movable-do solfege: A system where "do" always equals the tonic of the current key.
🎹 PART 2 · MOVABLE-DO SOLFEGE

In movable-do solfege, the syllable "do" always represents the tonic of whatever key you are in. This makes the system transposable: once you know what do-re-mi sounds like, you can sing in any key by placing "do" on the correct tonic pitch. The syllables carry the intervallic relationships with them.

You have been using major solfege throughout this course. Now the table below maps all seven scale degrees to their syllables and shows which syllables change in minor.

SOLFEGE LADDER: Major and Minor Scale Degrees
Degree Major Minor mod What changes
8 / 1 do do Tonic. No change.
7 ti te (natural minor) Lowered a half step in natural minor. Harmonic minor keeps ti.
6 la le Lowered a half step in natural and harmonic minor.
5 sol sol No change.
4 fa fa No change.
3 mi me Lowered a half step. This is the syllable that defines the minor sound.
2 re re No change.
1 do do Tonic. No change.
Green chips: major syllables and syllables that stay the same in minor. Purple chips: syllables that lower in minor forms.

Quick reference by scale form:

  • Major: do – re – mi – fa – sol – la – ti – do
  • Natural minor: do – re – me – fa – sol – le – te – do
  • Harmonic minor: do – re – me – fa – sol – le – ti – do
🎼 PART 3 · CURWEN-KODALY HAND SIGNS

The Curwen-Kodaly hand signs are physical gestures assigned to each solfege syllable. Developed by John Curwen in the 19th century and popularized by music educator Zoltan Kodaly, they add a kinesthetic (movement) dimension to pitch learning. When your hand, eye, ear, and voice all process the same pitch at once, the neural connections form faster.

The chart below shows the shape of each hand sign, from do at the bottom of the scale up to the next do. Study it, then use the flip cards to test yourself. You can also watch a hand-sign video in the resources section.

The Seven Hand Signs (do up to the next do)
⚡ TAP EACH CARD to see the hand sign position. Practice making the shape as you read.
🎺 HAND SIGN CARDS
do
Scale degree 1 (tap to see sign)
do: Fist held close to the waist, knuckles forward. Firm and grounded. This is the tonic anchor position.
re
Scale degree 2 (tap to see sign)
re: Fingers pointing outward and slightly downward, wrist angled away. Moving away from the tonic.
mi
Scale degree 3 (tap to see sign)
mi: Flat hand held sideways, palm facing the ground, fingers together. Horizontal and steady.
fa
Scale degree 4 (tap to see sign)
fa: Thumb pointing downward, other fingers curled or relaxed. The only syllable with the thumb pointing down.
sol
Scale degree 5 (tap to see sign)
sol: Flat open hand pointing outward at chest height, palm down, fingers spread. Open and confident.
la
Scale degree 6 (tap to see sign)
la: Hand relaxed, slightly lower than sol position, palm angled softly downward. Softer and drooping.
ti
Scale degree 7 (tap to see sign)
ti: Index finger points upward, the rest of the hand a loose fist. Ti pulls strongly up toward do.
do'
Scale degree 8, the octave (tap to see sign)
do (octave): The same fist as do, but held higher, up near the top of your reach. Same shape, one octave up.
🎼 PART 4 · FIVE STEPS TO SIGHT-SING A NEW MELODY

Before singing any new melody, follow this preparation sequence. The goal is to hear the melody in your inner ear before any sound leaves your mouth.

📝 NOTEBOOK FIRST
In your notebook, write down the five steps for preparing to sight-sing in your own words. Use the headings and content in the process card below as your guide. Write all five steps before opening anything.
5 STEPS TO SIGHT-SING A NEW MELODY
1
Find "do"
Identify the key and mode. Decide if it is major or minor. Assign "do" to the tonic pitch.
2
Feel the beat
Establish the tempo in your mind. Tap or conduct briefly before you sing a single note.
3
Scan for challenges
Look ahead for large leaps, chromatic notes, or tricky rhythms. Plan those moments before you reach them.
4
Identify patterns
Look for sequences, repeated motives, or scale runs. Recognizing patterns makes reading faster and more accurate.
5
SING. Keep going.
Maintain a steady tempo. Do not stop to fix individual wrong notes. Stopping defeats the purpose. Real sight-singing is real-time reading.

💪 THE ONE THING TO REMEMBER

In movable-do solfege, "do" is always the tonic. The minor modifications are: me for the lowered 3rd, le for the lowered 6th, te for the lowered 7th. When you sight-sing: find do, feel the beat, scan ahead, spot patterns, and then keep going without stopping to fix mistakes. Audiation grows with every real attempt. A little practice every day builds the skill faster than one long session.
