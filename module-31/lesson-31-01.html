LESSON 31.01
How Songs Are Built: Form and Harmony in Pop Music
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why do so many hit songs feel familiar even the first time you hear them?
Pop music is built from a small set of repeating blueprints: a handful of formal sections that appear in almost every chart-topping song, and a few chord progressions used so often that your brain has already memorized the sound. Once you can name those blueprints, you will hear the structure underneath any song you listen to.

3

🎵 PART 1 · FORMAL SECTIONS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN

The sections of a pop song

Every section in a pop song has a job. Here are the main ones:

  • Intro: Opens the song, often instrumental. Sets the mood before any singing starts.
  • Verse: Tells the story. The words change each time, moving the narrative forward. Energy is usually lower here.
  • Pre-chorus: A short section that builds tension between the verse and the chorus. Not every song has one.
  • Chorus: The emotional peak. Higher energy, most-repeated lyrics, the part everyone sings along to. The words stay the same every time it comes back.
  • Bridge: A contrasting section that breaks the verse-chorus pattern before the final chorus. It gives the song a change of scene.
  • Outro: Closes the song, often by fading out or winding down the energy.

🎹 PART 2 · HARMONIC SCHEMAS

Why certain chord progressions sound familiar

A harmonic schema is a chord progression used so often that it functions like a template. When a composer picks one of these schemas, listeners already know the sound before they have heard the specific song.

The most common schema in pop is the I-V-vi-IV loop (often called the "four-chord loop"). In the key of C major those chords are C - G - Am - F. The loop just keeps repeating under the whole song.

A second classic schema is the I-vi-IV-V, also called the "50s doo-wop" progression. In C major that is C - Am - F - G. You hear this one in countless songs from the 1950s through today.

One important legal note: chord progressions cannot be protected by copyright. Only a specific melody, lyric, and recording are legally protected. That means two different songs can share the exact same chord progression with no legal issue at all.

🎻 PART 3 · COPYRIGHT AND YOUR ANALYSIS

What copyright protects (and what it does not)

Copyright law protects a specific combination of melody, lyrics, and recording. It does not protect a chord progression, a time signature, or a key. That is why you can describe the harmonic schema of any song without reproducing it: the sequence I-V-vi-IV is a description of a pattern, not the song itself. Different artists can legally use the exact same chord progression.

For your analysis work, the rule is straightforward: link out to a legal recording (Spotify, YouTube, Apple Music), describe what you hear in your own words, and do not paste lyrics or copy out notation. Describing form and harmony is the job of a music theorist; reproducing someone else's creative expression is not.

💪 THE ONE THING TO REMEMBER

Pop songs are built from a small toolkit: a handful of repeating sections (verse, chorus, bridge) and a handful of repeating chord schemas (I-V-vi-IV, I-vi-IV-V). The chorus is the emotional peak; the verse tells the story; the bridge gives contrast. Chord progressions are not copyrightable, so describing them is exactly what music theorists do. The next time you hear a song, you have the vocabulary to describe what is happening.
