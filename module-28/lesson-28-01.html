LESSON 28.01
Pentatonic Scales: The Five-Note Scale
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why do so many musical traditions around the world, separated by thousands of miles, arrive at the same five-note scale?
From Scottish bagpipes to West African kora to blues guitar, five particular notes keep showing up together. That is not a coincidence. Today you will find out exactly which five notes they are, why they work so well together, and how to use them to create your own melody.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify the notes of the major and minor pentatonic scales by their scale degrees.
2
I will be able to explain why pentatonic scales have no strong tension or pull.
3
I will be able to create a short pentatonic melody.
🎵 PART 1 · WHAT IS A PENTATONIC SCALE?

Pentatonic means five notes: penta = five; tonic = tone. There are two main types used across Western and world music: the major pentatonic and the minor pentatonic.

The major pentatonic uses scale degrees 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6. That means you take a regular major scale and remove the 4th and the 7th. In the key of C, that gives you: C, D, E, G, A.

The minor pentatonic uses scale degrees 1, flat‑3, 4, 5, and flat‑7. In the key of A, that gives you: A, C, D, E, G.

MAJOR PENTATONIC
Degrees: 1-2-3-5-6
In C: C, D, E, G, A
MINOR PENTATONIC
Degrees: 1-flat‑3-4-5-flat‑7
In A: A, C, D, E, G

Here is the key idea: removing the 4th and the 7th removes the two "pull" notes in a major scale. The 7th wants to resolve up to the tonic; the 4th wants to resolve down. Without those two pull tones, the scale feels open and free, with no strong tension to resolve.

C Major Pentatonic: Notes on the Keyboard
What is removed: the 4th (F) and the 7th (B). Without those two pull-notes, the scale feels open and free. The five green keys are all you need.
🎹 PART 2 · THE BLACK-KEY SHORTCUT

Here is a shortcut that makes pentatonic scales easy to find on any keyboard: the five black keys of a piano form a pentatonic scale. From left to right they are G♯, A♯, C♯, D♯, and F♯ (or spelled with flats: A♭, B♭, D♭, E♭, G♭).

Slide your finger across all five black keys: that is a pentatonic scale. There are no wrong notes. Any combination of those five keys will sound musical together, which is why pentatonic is a great starting point for improvisation.

🌎 PENTATONIC ACROSS CULTURES
Different cultures, same five-note structure: the West African kora, Scottish bagpipes, the Chinese guqin, blues guitar, and country music all rely on pentatonic scales. The scale is not owned by any one tradition. It keeps appearing because it is easy to sing, easy to harmonize, and sounds complete without resolving tension.

🎼 PART 3 · USING PENTATONIC FOR MELODY AND IMPROVISATION

Because there are no strong dissonances in the pentatonic scale, a pentatonic melody sounds complete even if you stop without resolving any tension. The notes simply rest.

Many familiar pop melodies are built largely on the major pentatonic. Listen to how few different pitches some of the most recognizable songs use. You can search for listening examples here: YouTube: pentatonic scale melody examples.

Rock and blues solos most often use the minor pentatonic. The characteristic sound of electric guitar improvisation comes almost entirely from moving around those five notes. To hear this in action, listen to some examples: YouTube: pentatonic improvisation tutorial.

🎶 SUMMARY: TWO PENTATONIC TYPES
Major pentatonic (1-2-3-5-6): bright, open, uplifting. Common in folk, pop, gospel.
Minor pentatonic (1-flat‑3-4-5-flat‑7): darker, bluesy, expressive. Common in blues, rock, and R&B.

💪 THE ONE THING TO REMEMBER

The pentatonic scale removes the tension notes and leaves five pitches that always sound at home together. Major pentatonic (1-2-3-5-6) has a bright, open feel; minor pentatonic (1-flat‑3-4-5-flat‑7) has a darker, bluesy feel. Every time you slide across the five black keys on a piano, you are playing a pentatonic scale.
