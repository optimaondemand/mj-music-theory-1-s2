📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
How did a musical form born in the Mississippi Delta become the foundation of rock, pop, and jazz worldwide?
The blues is not just a mood. It is a precise musical system: a 12-bar form, a specific set of chords, a characteristic scale, and a lyric structure. Once you understand the system, you will recognize it in hundreds of songs you already know.

🎵 PART 1 · THE ORIGINS OF THE BLUES

The blues emerged from African-American communities in the Mississippi Delta in the late 1800s and early 1900s. It grew from work songs, spirituals, and field hollers that enslaved people and their descendants used to communicate and process experience.

The Delta blues tradition (artists like Robert Johnson and Charley Patton) moved north with the Great Migration. In Chicago by the 1940s and 1950s it became electric, with artists like Muddy Waters and Howlin’ Wolf leading the way. From there it fed directly into rock and roll (Chuck Berry, Elvis Presley) and British rock (The Rolling Stones, Led Zeppelin). All of these figures are historical; use the link below to hear what the tradition sounded like at the start.

The blues is not just a feeling; it is a specific musical system: a 12-measure form, a specific set of chords, a characteristic scale, and a lyric structure. Learning that system is this lesson’s goal.

The 12-Bar Blues Form

The 12-bar blues is a repeating 12-measure cycle. Three chords fill those 12 bars in a specific pattern every time.

I = the tonic chord; IV = the subdominant; V = the dominant.
In G blues: I7 = G7, IV7 = C7, V7 = D7. The pattern is the same in any key.
♫ PART 2 · DOMINANT 7TH CHORDS IN THE BLUES

In standard harmony, dominant 7th chords (like G7) normally appear only on the V chord and pull toward the tonic. In blues, you put a dominant 7th on all three chords: I7, IV7, and V7.

A dominant 7th chord = a major triad plus a flat-7. In G7: G – B – D – F. The flat-7 is F natural, not F♯.

💡 KEY IDEA
Using dominant 7th chords on all three scale degrees gives the blues its characteristic tense, unresolved quality, even on the home chord. Nothing fully resolves. That is the sound.
🎼 PART 3 · THE BLUES SCALE

Start with the minor pentatonic: 1 – ♭3 – 4 – 5 – ♭7. Now add one more note between the 4th and the 5th: the ♭5. This note is called the blue note.

Blues scale in A: A – C – D – E♭ – E – G. The E♭ is the ♭5 blue note.

The blue note creates an intense, bending sound. Blues guitarists often bend the string between E♭ and E, sliding between them. That tension is what gives the blues its characteristic ache.

🎤 PART 4 · AAB LYRIC PHRASING AND CALL-AND-RESPONSE

Traditional blues lyrics follow an AAB pattern: the first line states an idea (A), the second line repeats it (A), and the third line delivers the conclusion or twist (B). This matches the 12-bar form: the AA covers bars 1–8 and the B covers bars 9–12.

AAB EXAMPLE (original example for this lesson)
A “I woke up this morning, the sky was turning gray.”
A “I said I woke up this morning, and the sky was turning gray.”
B “I picked up my old guitar and started to play.”

Call-and-response: in the blues, a vocal phrase (the call) is answered by a guitar or harmonica lick (the response). This mirrors African music traditions. It is one reason blues feels like a conversation rather than a solo performance.

(tap to flip)
The turnaround returns to the I7 chord and leads the music back to the top of the 12-bar form. It keeps the cycle going.

💪 WHAT TO TAKE WITH YOU

The 12-bar blues is one of the most repeated musical forms in history. Twelve bars, three chords (all dominant 7ths), a scale built on the minor pentatonic plus one blue note, and lyrics that say the hard thing twice before resolving it. Every time you recognize this form, you are hearing the foundation of modern American music.
