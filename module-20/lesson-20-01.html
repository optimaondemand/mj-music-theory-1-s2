📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How do musicians write down what they hear?
You have read rhythms and performed them. Today you go in the opposite direction: you listen to rhythm and write it down. You will also learn two of the most interesting rhythmic effects in music -- hemiola and cross-rhythm. Both involve layers of rhythm that pull against each other. Once you hear them, you start noticing them everywhere.

🎵 PART 1 · RHYTHMIC DICTATION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think about music you know well. Has a rhythm ever surprised you -- shifted your sense of where the beat was? (No wrong answer.)

Rhythmic dictation is the skill of listening to a rhythm and writing it down accurately in standard notation. It is the rhythmic counterpart to pitch dictation. Together, these two skills form the core of ear training. Good rhythmic dictation ability develops your musical memory, your sense of meter, and your understanding of rhythmic notation.

RHYTHMIC DICTATION: 6-STEP PROCESS
Step 1
Listen once without writing. Feel the meter.
Step 2
Listen again: tap the beat. Identify simple vs. compound meter.
Step 3
Listen again: focus on one measure at a time.
Step 4
Write measure by measure. Start from the first measure.
Step 5
Check: does each measure total correctly before moving on?
Step 6
Listen one more time: verify your notation matches what you hear.

Common challenges: syncopated rhythms land on normally weak beats; fast sixteenth-note patterns require careful listening; ties across barlines can look confusing even when they sound clear. With practice your ear will learn to parse these automatically.

🎼 PART 2 · HEMIOLA

Hemiola is a rhythmic device in which three groups of two beats are temporarily felt as two groups of three beats (or the reverse). The word comes from the Greek for "1.5:1 ratio." Hemiola creates a striking effect: the listener's sense of where the downbeat falls seems to shift.

The classic example: in 3/4 time, a hemiola spans two measures, subdividing the six beats as 2+2+2 instead of 3+3. This makes two measures of triple meter sound like one measure of duple meter. Baroque composers used hemiola frequently at cadence points -- it slows the harmonic rhythm and creates emphasis just before a final resolution. You can hear famous hemiola examples in Handel's "Hallelujah" chorus and in numerous Baroque dances.

💡 WHERE TO HEAR HEMIOLA
Hemiola is most common in: Baroque dances (Handel, Bach), Classical minuets, Brahms symphonies, and many folk and dance traditions. It almost always appears at a cadence point -- just before the final resolution of a phrase. Listen for it in Handel's Hallelujah chorus (link in resources).
🎼 PART 3 · CROSS-RHYTHM AND POLYRHYTHM

A cross-rhythm occurs when two contrasting rhythmic patterns are layered simultaneously, creating tension between different metric divisions. The simplest example is 3 against 2: one voice plays three notes in the same span that another voice plays two notes. This creates a characteristic "push-pull" tension.

Cross-rhythms are foundational to West African drumming traditions, Latin music (including the clave rhythm), jazz, and much contemporary classical music. Polyrhythm is the simultaneous use of two or more different metric patterns -- 3 against 2, 4 against 3, 5 against 4. The best way to experience this: pat your knee with one hand in groups of 2 while tapping your other hand in groups of 3 against the same overall pulse.

Hand 1 divides 6 units into 2 groups of 3. Hand 2 divides the same 6 units into 3 groups of 2. Both share unit 1 as a common landing point. The pull between them is the cross-rhythm. Try it: left hand taps on 1 and 4; right hand taps on 1, 3, and 5.
💡 CROSS-RHYTHMS IN MUSIC
Cross-rhythms are most common in: West African drumming, Latin music (clave), jazz (swing feel), and 20th/21st century classical music. Musicians who understand polyrhythm have a significant advantage in all of these styles.

See a sample hemiola rhythm.
One common hemiola notation: two dotted half notes written across the barline of two 3/4 measures. Each dotted half note lasts 3 beats. The first one starts on beat 1 of measure 1. The second one starts on beat 1 of measure 2. Written this way, the accent pattern becomes 1+1+1+1+1+1 of dotted quarters, or -- more commonly -- three note attacks that span beats 1, 3, and 5 of the six-beat span (using ties or dotted notes).

Another common notation: in two measures of 3/4, write three dotted quarter notes (three beats each) instead of the normal pattern of three quarter notes per measure. The dotted quarters land on beats 1, 3, and 5 of the six-beat span. Because beats 3 and 5 cut across the normal barline groupings, the listener hears three equal groups of 2 instead of two groups of 3.

💪 THE ONE THING TO REMEMBER

For rhythmic dictation: listen first, then write measure by measure, then verify. Hemiola takes two 3/4 measures (6 beats) and regroups them 2+2+2. Cross-rhythm layers two different metric divisions over the same pulse. Both hemiola and cross-rhythm create deliberate rhythmic tension. Once you can name them, you hear them everywhere.
