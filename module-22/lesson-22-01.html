Borrowed Pull: Secondary Dominants and Tonicization
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What happens when a chord briefly pretends it is the tonic of a different key?
You have heard progressions that suddenly feel more tense or brighter for just one chord. Often that moment is a secondary dominant: a chord that borrows the "pull power" of a dominant seventh and points it at a non-tonic chord. This lesson explains how that works and how to spot it by name.

🎵 PART 1 · THE BIG IDEA: TONICIZATION

One chord, borrowed from another key

You already know that a dominant seventh chord creates a strong pull toward the tonic. That pull comes from the interval of a tritone inside the chord: it wants to resolve. Tonicization uses that same pull but aims it at a chord other than the real tonic.

In C major, for example, the chord G (V) normally pulls to C (I). But what if you briefly treated G as a temporary "home base"? You would approach it with its own dominant: D major (or D7). D does not belong to C major (it contains F♯, which is not in the C major scale). But that temporary D chord makes G feel like a local tonic for one brief moment, before the music returns to C major.

That is tonicization: a brief, temporary treatment of a non-tonic chord as a local tonic. It usually lasts just one or two chords. It is not a full modulation, which requires a sustained change of key.

🔎 CUE CARD · HOW TO SPOT A SECONDARY DOMINANT
Step 1
See a chord with an accidental (a sharp or flat not in the key signature)?
Step 2
Is it a major triad or dominant seventh chord? (Diminished and minor chords rarely act as secondary dominants.)
Step 3
Drop down a perfect 5th. What chord do you land on? That is its target.
Step 4
Does the target chord exist diatonically in the current key? If yes: secondary dominant confirmed. Label it V/X.

🎹 PART 2 · THE V/X NOTATION

Reading and writing secondary dominant labels

The label V/X is read "five of X." The X is any diatonic chord that is being temporarily tonicized. So V/V means "the dominant of the dominant," V/IV means "the dominant of the subdominant," and so on.

Label Read as In C major (notes) Resolves to
V/V five of five D–F♯–A (or D7) G (V)
V/IV five of four C–E–G–B♭ (C7) F (IV)
V/ii five of two A–C♯–E (or A7) Dm (ii)
V/vi five of six E–G♯–B (or E7) Am (vi)

Notice that every row in the table has at least one chromatic note (a note with an accidental). That is the key signal. In C major, any chord with an F♯, C♯, G♯, or B♭ is a likely secondary dominant.

💡 THE KEY RULE
A secondary dominant always contains at least one note that does not belong to the current key. Spot the accidental, ask "where does this resolve?" and you have found your secondary dominant.

🎼 PART 3 · HEARING AND ANALYZING SECONDARY DOMINANTS

Why do they sound the way they do?

Secondary dominants work because your ear has learned to expect a dominant seventh chord to resolve down a perfect fifth. When a secondary dominant appears, your ear automatically hears the target chord as a temporary home. The music gains direction and energy for just a moment, then returns to the main key.

The progression V/V → V → I (for example, D7 → G → C in C major) is one of the most common and satisfying harmonic moves in classical music, pop, and jazz. The D7 intensifies the pull to G, making the final resolution to C feel even stronger.

Notice the F♯ in D7. That note is not in C major, so this chord is borrowed. It acts like the dominant of G, so we label it V/V. Like any dominant, it resolves down a perfect fifth, landing on G (the real V), which then leads home to C. That single chromatic note is your clue to spot a secondary dominant.

The rule of thumb: look for a chord with a sharp or flat that does not belong to the key. If that chord is major or a dominant seventh and it resolves down a perfect fifth to a diatonic chord, it is a secondary dominant. If you have a keyboard tool handy, play the progression above and listen for the moment of extra pull on the D7.

💪 THE ONE THING TO REMEMBER

A secondary dominant borrows the dominant's pull and points it at a non-tonic chord. Look for a chromatic accidental: if the chord is major or dominant seventh and resolves down a fifth to a diatonic chord, it is a secondary dominant. Label it V/X where X is the chord it resolves to. The most common is V/V, which makes the dominant feel even more like home.
