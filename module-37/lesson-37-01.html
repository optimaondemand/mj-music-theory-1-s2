🎵 PART 1 · THE YEAR AS A SPIRAL

Music Theory I did not move in a straight line. It moved in a spiral: each quarter returned to the same fundamental ideas (pitch, rhythm, harmony, listening) at a higher level of detail and skill. Here is how the four quarters connect.

Quarter 1 built the foundation. You learned how music is written (staff, clefs, rhythm), what pitches are and how they relate to each other (scales, intervals, triads), and how to name and classify what you hear. Without Q1, nothing else has a name.

Quarter 2 deepened that foundation into harmony. You learned how chords function in a key (diatonic harmony, Roman numerals, chord progressions), how minor keys differ from major, and how composers organize music across time (form). Q2 took the building blocks of Q1 and showed you how to connect them.

Quarter 3 trained your ear and widened your view. Ear training asked you to hear what you had been reading: to identify intervals, chords, and melodic patterns by sound alone. Music history placed what you learned in time: you heard how composers from different eras used the same theory tools differently. Counterpoint, species rules, and world music broadened the picture beyond any single tradition. Q3 turned head knowledge into listening knowledge.

Quarter 4 applied everything. Composition asked you to use pitch, rhythm, harmony, form, ear, and history together. You did not just analyze: you made something. Music technology showed you how the tools around you connect to the theory inside you. And now, at Module 37, you review and reflect: which ideas became part of how you hear music?

Q1
Foundations: Notation, Rhythm, Scales, Intervals, Triads
  • Staff, clefs, note values
  • Major scale construction
  • Interval identification
  • Major, minor, diminished triads
Q2
Harmony: Diatonic Chords, Minor Keys, Form
  • Roman numeral analysis
  • Chord progressions (I–IV–V–I)
  • Natural, harmonic, melodic minor
  • Binary and ternary form
Q3
Ear and History: Ear Training, Music History, Counterpoint, World Music
  • Interval and chord recognition by ear
  • Baroque through Modern eras
  • Species counterpoint rules
  • Non-Western pitch and rhythm systems
Q4
Applied Music: Composition, Technology, Synthesis
  • Original melody and composition
  • DAW, MIDI, and music technology
  • Year-end review across all quarters
  • Portfolio and reflection
Each quarter built on the one before. Theory is a spiral, not a checklist.
🎵 PART 2 · Q1 SPIRAL REVIEW

Key ideas: The staff and clefs give pitches their names. The major scale is a pattern of whole and half steps. Intervals measure distance. Triads stack thirds.

✎ ASK YOURSELF
Can I still name the notes in a C major scale? A G major scale? Can I name an interval by sight and by ear? Can I identify a major, minor, or diminished triad?
🎵 PART 3 · Q2 SPIRAL REVIEW

Key ideas: Roman numerals describe chord function in a key. The I chord sounds like home; V sounds like it needs to resolve; IV adds richness. The natural, harmonic, and melodic minor scales each have a specific character. Binary and ternary form describe the shape of a piece.

✎ ASK YOURSELF
Can I write a I–IV–V–I progression in any key? Can I describe what makes minor sound "minor"? Can I identify ABA form when I hear it?
🎵 PART 4 · Q3 SPIRAL REVIEW

Key ideas: Intervals sound different, and ear training teaches you to hear which is which. Composers from the Baroque through the Modern era used the same theory tools for very different goals. Species counterpoint gives rules for two independent voices; the rules point to one goal: voices that move well together. Non-Western music systems organize pitch and rhythm in ways that are genuinely different from (not inferior to) Western conventions.

✎ ASK YOURSELF
Can I sing a perfect fifth in tune? Can I identify at least one composer per era? Do I understand why "do not make parallel fifths" is a rule?
🎵 PART 5 · Q4 SPIRAL REVIEW AND GROWTH

Key ideas: Composing forces you to make decisions, not just identify them. A strong original melody has a clear shape: it rises and falls, uses the chord tones of the key, and has a recognizable rhythm. Technology does not replace theory; it gives theory tools to work with sound directly.

✎ ASK YOURSELF
What was the hardest moment in my original composition? What would I do differently? Which music theory concept am I most curious to keep exploring?
⚡ CHECK IN WITH YOURSELF
How are you feeling right now, looking back at the full year? (No wrong answer.)

💪 THE ONE THING TO REMEMBER

Theory is a spiral. Q1 named the notes. Q2 connected them into harmony. Q3 trained your ear and widened your view. Q4 put everything into practice. You did not just study music: you developed a way of hearing it. That stays with you.
