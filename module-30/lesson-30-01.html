📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why do jazz musicians from New York to Tokyo, from the 1940s to today, keep returning to the same three-chord pattern?
Jazz has thousands of songs. But underneath almost all of them is one pattern: a minor seventh chord moving to a tense dominant seventh chord, which then lands on a bright major seventh chord. Once you learn to spot it, you will hear it in jazz ballads, bebop tunes, and even pop and R&B. In this lesson you learn the pattern, how to read chord symbols on a lead sheet, and what gives jazz its characteristic rolling rhythm.

🎵 PART 1 · THE ii-V-I PROGRESSION

The cornerstone of jazz harmony

The ii-V-I is the most important three-chord pattern in jazz. In any major key, the formula is: iimi7 – V7 – Imaj7.

In C major: Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7. In F major: Gm7 – C7 – Fmaj7. The Roman numerals tell you the scale degree of each chord root.

Here is what each chord does:

  • The iimi7 has a minor, questioning quality. It sets up motion away from home.
  • The V7 builds strong tension. It contains a tritone between its 3rd and 7th that pulls toward resolution.
  • The Imaj7 resolves that tension to a bright, settled sound. This is home.

A jazz standard often moves through several keys, each with its own ii-V-I. Learning to spot these three-chord chains unlocks most of the jazz repertoire.

🎼 PART 2 · READING CHORD SYMBOLS

The shorthand of jazz

Chord symbols are a shorthand system. Each symbol tells you the root note and the chord quality. Once you know the code, you can read a jazz lead sheet from any publisher in the world without asking what each symbol means.

Here are the five types you need to know for jazz:

  • Cmaj7: major triad + major 7th (C – E – G – B). Bright, settled.
  • C7: major triad + flat-7 (C – E – G – B♭). Tense; pulls toward resolution.
  • Cmi7: minor triad + flat-7 (C – E♭ – G – B♭). Dark, floating quality.
  • Cdim7: four stacked minor thirds (C – E♭ – G♭ – B♭♭). Maximum tension.
  • Cmi7(♭5): minor 7th with a flat-5 (C – E♭ – G♭ – B♭). Also written CØ7. Ambiguous and tense; it is the ii chord in a minor-key ii-V-I.

🎹 PART 3 · SWING RHYTHM

What makes jazz feel like jazz

Jazz uses a distinctive feel called swing: eighth notes are not played evenly. Instead of two equal notes per beat, the first eighth note is long and the second is short. The ratio is roughly 2 to 1, similar to a dotted eighth followed by a sixteenth, or like landing on beat 1 and the third part of a triplet.

This contrast is called swung eighths versus straight eighths. Swing creates a lilting, rolling, forward-leaning feeling that straight playing does not have.

Straight eighths: all four notes equal. Swing eighths: beat lands long, the "and" is short. Ratio is roughly 2:1.
Search YouTube: swing vs straight eighth notes jazz
💡 HOW TO HEAR IT
When you listen, focus on the "and" of each beat. In straight playing it is an even split. In swing it is clipped and short. That short "and" is what makes the rhythm roll forward. Jazz musicians learn to feel this rather than count it, but knowing the 2:1 ratio helps you understand what your ear is already picking up.

🎤 MUSIC CAREERS

A jazz musician brings a chart to life in real time, adding improvisation on top of the written chords. The path from a composer's idea to a listener's ears runs through many roles: the composer writes the chart, publishers and copyists distribute it, musicians learn the changes, a bandleader hires the players, and a venue or record label produces the performance. Every one of those steps is a career in music.

💪 THE THREE THINGS TO REMEMBER

The ii-V-I is a three-chord engine that drives most jazz harmony: minor seventh, dominant seventh, major seventh. Chord symbols are the shorthand that lets musicians from any background read music together quickly. Swing rhythm is the long-short eighth-note feel that turns written notes into jazz. Learn these three things and you can walk into almost any jazz session and follow along.
